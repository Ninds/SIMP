\section*{Before we begin}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Before we begin}
You have already met the action, the calculus of variations, and the Euler--Lagrange equation. You know that a Lagrangian can be written as $L=T-V$ for an ordinary conservative mechanical system, or as $T+W$ when $W=-V$ is the appropriate \emph{work potential} (up to an irrelevant constant).

This tutorial takes that understanding as its starting point. Its goal is not to make you fluent in advanced calculations. It is to make Hamiltonian mechanics feel \emph{inevitable} rather than an arbitrary change of symbols.

\begin{intuition}
The whole journey fits into one chain of thoughts:
\[
\boxed{\text{action}\ \longrightarrow\ L(q,\dot q)\ \longrightarrow\ p=\pd{L}{\dot q}\ \longrightarrow\ H(q,p)\ \longrightarrow\ (\dot q,\dot p).}
\]
We shall spend most of our time on the \emph{why} behind the middle arrows.
\end{intuition}

\textbf{A word about notation.} We first study one coordinate $q(t)$, with velocity $\dot q=\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt$. Later, $q$ may mean an angle or any other \emph{generalised coordinate}: it need not be a distance. The symbol $p$ will always mean the momentum \emph{conjugate to that coordinate}.

\section{Why invent another way to describe motion?}
\subsection{A familiar question, asked in a different form}
Imagine a particle travelling along a line in a potential $V(q)$. Newton's law says
\[
 m\ddot q=-V'(q).
\]
This is a \emph{second-order} equation for position. To predict the future, we must know both the present position and velocity.

But we can rewrite exactly the same motion as a pair of \emph{first-order} equations. Define the familiar momentum $p=m\dot q$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newtonpair}
 \boxed{\dot q=\frac{p}{m},\qquad \dot p=-V'(q).}
\end{equation}
The first equation answers: \emph{how does position change?} The second answers: \emph{how does momentum change?}

Nothing new has happened to the physics. We have merely described a moving state by a \emph{pair} $(q,p)$ instead of by a second-order equation for $q$ alone.

\subsection{The quantity that gives both answers}
For this particular system, its energy is
\[
 E=T+V=\tfrac12m\dot q^2+V(q)
       =\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q).
\]
For a moment, call this expression $H(q,p)$. Do two simple differentiations:
\[
 \pd{H}{p}=\frac{p}{m},\qquad \pd{H}{q}=V'(q).
\]
Compare these with the two equations in \eqref{eq:newtonpair}. We have \emph{discovered}, before introducing any new formalism, that
\begin{keybox}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preview}
 \boxed{\dot q=\pd{H}{p},\qquad
 \dot p=-\pd{H}{q}.}
\end{equation}
These derivatives are not initially mysterious. In the simplest example, one derivative gives the velocity; the other gives minus the force.
\end{keybox}

This is the seed of Hamiltonian mechanics. A single function $H(q,p)$ supplies \emph{both} directions of change.

\begin{pausebox}
Why not write $E(q,\dot q)=\tfrac12m\dot q^2+V(q)$ and differentiate with respect to velocity? Because $\partial E/\partial\dot q=m\dot q=p$, \emph{not} $\dot q$. If we want the derivative of our new function to give velocity, $p$ is the more natural input variable.
\end{pausebox}

\subsection{A state is now a point in a plane}
Every pair $(q,p)$ is one possible instantaneous state, represented by a point in \emph{phase space}. As the system evolves, the point traces a curve. Position and momentum are treated as independent coordinates for the purpose of describing the \emph{current state}; the equations then tell us how that point moves.

This language becomes especially powerful when there are several coordinates, constraints, or interactions. We will return to phase-space pictures after deriving the general equations.

\section{Why momentum rather than velocity?}
\subsection{What the action was already trying to tell us}
Recall the action for one coordinate:
\[
 S[q]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(q,\dot q,t)\dd t.
\]
Perturb the path slightly: $q(t)\mapsto q(t)+\varepsilon\eta(t)$, so that $\dot q\mapsto\dot q+\varepsilon\dot\eta$.

To first order, the change in the action is
\[
 \delta S=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
 \left(\pd{L}{q}\,\eta+\pd{L}{\dot q}\,\dot\eta\right)\dd t.
\]
Notice the factor multiplying the change in \emph{velocity}:
\[
 \boxed{\pd{L}{\dot q}.}
\]
It appears naturally, without us having chosen any new definition. The action itself is inviting us to give this quantity a name.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Definition (canonical or conjugate momentum).} For a coordinate $q$ with Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momentum}
 \boxed{p:=\pd{L}{\dot q}.}
\end{equation}
For a conventional particle with $L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2-V(q)$, this reduces to $p=m\dot q$.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Momentum is the sensitivity of the Lagrangian to velocity}
Hold position and time fixed, and make a tiny change in velocity. Then
\[
 \Delta L\approx\pd{L}{\dot q}\,\Delta\dot q=p\,\Delta\dot q.
\]
So $p$ measures how strongly the Lagrangian responds to a small change in velocity. This is a subtler idea than simply memorising $p=m\dot q$.

It is also why $p$ can be meaningful even when $q$ is not a distance and $\dot q$ is not an ordinary linear velocity.

\subsection{The Euler--Lagrange equation is already an equation for momentum}
The integration-by-parts step you know from the calculus of variations gives
\[
 \delta S=
 \left[\pd{L}{\dot q}\,\eta\right]_{t_0}^{t_1}
 +\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
 \left(\pd{L}{q}-\dt{}\left(\pd{L}{\dot q}\right)\right)\eta\dd t.
\]
For fixed endpoints the boundary term vanishes. Requiring $\delta S=0$ leads to
\[
 \dt{}\left(\pd{L}{\dot q}\right)=\pd{L}{q}.
\]
Now use the natural name $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eulerp}
 \boxed{\dot p=\pd{L}{q}.}
\end{equation}

\begin{intuition}
The move from velocity to momentum is \emph{not} something Hamilton invented out of nowhere. The Euler--Lagrange equation is already telling us what changes in time: it is $\partial L/\partial\dot q$. Hamiltonian mechanics takes that quantity seriously as a variable in its own right.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{A striking example: moving around a circle}
Suppose a particle of mass $m$ is constrained to move on a circle of radius $R$, described by its angle $\theta$. Its speed is $R\dot\theta$, and
\[
 L(\theta,\dot\theta)=\tfrac12mR^2\dot\theta^2-V(\theta).
\]
The momentum conjugate to $\theta$ is
\[
 \boxed{p_\theta=\pd{L}{\dot\theta}=mR^2\dot\theta=I\dot\theta,\qquad I=mR^2.}
\]
This is \emph{angular momentum}, not $m\dot\theta$. That is exactly as it should be: $\theta$ is an angle, so its conjugate momentum is the momentum associated with rotation.

More generally, different coordinates can lead to different kinds of conjugate momentum. The universal definition $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$ adapts automatically.

\begin{pausebox}
What is the conjugate momentum for $L=\tfrac12a(q)\dot q^2-V(q)$, where $a(q)>0$? It is $p=a(q)\dot q$, not necessarily a constant multiple of velocity. We will use this example later.
\end{pausebox}

\subsection{There is also a boundary interpretation}
If we do \emph{not} hold the endpoints of the varied path fixed, the first-order variation along a true physical path reduces to
\[
 \delta S_{\mathrm{on\ shell}}=[p\,\delta q]_{t_0}^{t_1}.
\]
With the initial endpoint fixed, a tiny displacement of the final endpoint changes the physical action by approximately $p(t_1)\,\delta q(t_1)$. Momentum tells us how sensitive the action is to a displacement of the endpoint.

This observation is not needed to solve equations, but it gives the word \emph{conjugate} a deeper meaning: $p$ is naturally paired with changes in $q$.

\section{The next puzzle: how to build a function of $(q,p)$}
\subsection{We want to exchange velocity for momentum}
In Lagrangian mechanics we feed $L$ a position and a velocity: $L(q,\dot q,t)$. We now want a function that takes position and momentum: $H(q,p,t)$.

The first requirement is that we can recover the velocity from $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$. For a standard mass, $p=m\dot q$ gives $\dot q=p/m$.

When the Lagrangian has a sufficiently non-degenerate dependence on velocity, this inversion works locally. For several coordinates the relevant condition is that the velocity Hessian matrix $\bigl(\partial^2 L/\partial\dot q_i\partial\dot q_j\bigr)$ be invertible. Some constrained systems require extra machinery; we leave those aside.

\subsection{Why not just substitute $\dot q(p)$ into $L$?}
For our ordinary particle,
\[
 L=\frac12m\dot q^2-V(q),\qquad
 L\bigl(q,\dot q(p)\bigr)=\frac{p^2}{2m}-V(q).
\]
Differentiate this substituted expression with respect to $p$. It gives $p/m=\dot q$: promising! But its derivative with respect to $q$ is $-V'(q)$, while the desired momentum equation $\dot p=-V'(q)$ would require the \emph{negative} derivative of the new function to equal the force. We need a function whose $q$-derivative has the opposite sign from the $q$-derivative of $L$.

Indeed, simply replacing $\dot q$ by $p/m$ in $L$ does not produce the correct second equation. We need an adjustment, not a mere relabelling.

\subsection{Invent the adjustment from what we want its derivative to do}
For the moment, keep $q,t$ fixed and imagine $L$ as a curve against velocity $v=\dot q$. We know
\[
 p=\pd{L}{v}.
\]
The tangent line at the chosen velocity $v$ has slope $p$ and equation
\[
 \text{tangent}(u)=L(v)+p(u-v)=p\,u-\bigl(pv-L(v)\bigr).
\]
The \emph{negative of its vertical intercept} is $pv-L(v)$. This suggests a new quantity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:legendre}
 \boxed{H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-L(q,\dot q,t),\quad
       \dot q=\dot q(q,p,t).}
\end{equation}
This procedure is called a \emph{Legendre transform}. You do not need the name to understand its role: it swaps the variable $\dot q$ for the slope $p$ of the $L$-versus-velocity curve.

\begin{intuition}
The Legendre transform is not an arbitrary trick for changing a minus sign. It is the natural way to exchange a variable (velocity) for the slope associated with that variable (momentum), while retaining enough information to recover the original motion.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{See $T-V$ turn into $T+V$}
For $L=\frac12m\dot q^2-V(q)$ and $p=m\dot q$,
\begin{align*}
 H&=p\dot q-L\\
  &=m\dot q^2-\left(\frac12m\dot q^2-V\right)\\
  &=\frac12m\dot q^2+V\\
  &=\boxed{\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q)}.
\end{align*}
The change $T-V\to T+V$ is not inserted as a new axiom. It emerges from the Legendre transform. In this familiar class of systems, $H$ equals the total mechanical energy.

\begin{pausebox}
The Hamiltonian is not \emph{defined} as $T+V$. It is defined as $p\dot q-L$, with velocity eliminated in favour of $p$. It happens to equal $T+V$ in many ordinary systems. Keeping this distinction will prevent confusion later.
\end{pausebox}

\section{Why exactly are Hamilton's equations these two equations?}
\subsection{Start by asking what we need to predict next}
At any instant a state is $(q,p)$. To predict what happens an instant later, we need two rates of change:
\[
 (q,p)\ \longrightarrow\ \bigl(q+\dot q\,\Delta t,\ p+\dot p\,\Delta t\bigr).
\]
So Hamiltonian mechanics must provide $\dot q$ \emph{and} $\dot p$ from the current $(q,p)$. We have already seen, in the elementary particle example, that $H_p$ gives velocity and $-H_q$ gives force.

The question is: does this happen for \emph{any regular Lagrangian}, or was it a coincidence of $p=m\dot q$?

\subsection{Follow the small changes in $H$}
Write $v=\dot q$ temporarily to make the chain rule clearer. We have
\[
 H(q,p,t)=p\,v(q,p,t)-L\bigl(q,v(q,p,t),t\bigr).
\]
Consider a tiny change in $q$, $p$, and $t$. By the product rule and chain rule,
\begin{align*}
 \dd H
  &=v\dd p+p\dd v
       -\pd{L}{q}\dd q-\pd{L}{v}\dd v-\pd{L}{t}\dd t\\
  &=v\dd p+\underbrace{\left(p-\pd{L}{v}\right)}_{=\,0}\dd v
       -\pd{L}{q}\dd q-\pd{L}{t}\dd t.
\end{align*}
The terms involving $\dd v$ \emph{cancel exactly} because we defined $p=\partial L/\partial v$. This cancellation is the decisive reason for the Legendre-transform construction.

What remains is strikingly simple:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dh}
 \boxed{\dd H=v\dd p-\pd{L}{q}\dd q-\pd{L}{t}\dd t.}
\end{equation}
But because $H$ is now a function of $(q,p,t)$, its ordinary total differential is also
\[
 \dd H=\pd{H}{q}\dd q+\pd{H}{p}\dd p+\pd{H}{t}\dd t.
\]
Comparing coefficients gives
\[
 \pd{H}{p}=v=\dot q,\qquad
 \pd{H}{q}=-\pd{L}{q},\qquad
 \pd{H}{t}=-\pd{L}{t}.
\]
Finally the Euler--Lagrange equation told us $\dot p=\partial L/\partial q$. Therefore
\begin{keybox}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamilton}
 \boxed{\dot q=\pd{H}{p},\qquad\dot p=-\pd{H}{q}.}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Hamilton's equations are not a guess.} They are the Euler--Lagrange equation rewritten using the natural slope $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$ and the natural transformed function $H=p\dot q-L$.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{A physical reading of each derivative}
\begin{itemize}
 \item $\dot q=H_p$: how sensitively the Hamiltonian changes when you vary momentum at fixed position tells you how quickly position moves.
 \item $\dot p=-H_q$: how sensitively the Hamiltonian changes when you vary position at fixed momentum tells you how quickly the conjugate momentum changes, with a minus sign.
\end{itemize}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+V(q)$, the second relation becomes $\dot p=-V'(q)$, which is exactly the familiar force law. The negative sign reflects the way a conservative force points toward decreasing potential energy.

\begin{intuition}
The equations are \emph{crossed}: differentiate with respect to momentum to find the rate of change of position; differentiate with respect to position to find the rate of change of momentum. Their opposite signs make a remarkably symmetric pair. A displacement in either state variable drives change in the other.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{The many-coordinate version}
If there are generalised coordinates $q_1,\ldots,q_n$, define
\[
 p_i=\pd{L}{\dot q_i},\qquad
 H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i\dot q_i-L.
\]
Provided we can express all $\dot q_i$ in terms of $(q,p,t)$, the same cancellation works component by component:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:many}
 \boxed{\dot q_i=\pd{H}{p_i},\qquad
        \dot p_i=-\pd{H}{q_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.}
\end{equation}
A system with $n$ coordinates now has $2n$ first-order equations. The physics is unchanged, but its mathematical organisation has changed dramatically.

\section{The variational principle survives the change}
\subsection{A new-looking action, with the same physics}
Starting from $H=p\dot q-L$, rearrange:
\[
 L=p\dot q-H.
\]
The original action can therefore be written, along a path where $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$, as
\[
 S=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}[p\dot q-H(q,p,t)]\dd t.
\]
Now comes the interesting idea: regard $q(t)$ and $p(t)$ as \emph{independent functions to vary}. Define the \emph{phase-space action}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phaseaction}
 \boxed{S_H[q,p]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\bigl[p\dot q-H(q,p,t)\bigr]\dd t.}
\end{equation}
Why is $p$ allowed to vary independently? Because the equation obtained by varying $p$ will itself enforce the correct velocity--momentum relationship.

\subsection{Vary momentum first}
Let $p\mapsto p+\varepsilon\xi$, with $q$ held fixed. To first order,
\[
 \delta_p S_H=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\left(\dot q-\pd{H}{p}\right)\xi\dd t.
\]
If the action is stationary for arbitrary small $\xi(t)$, then
\[
 \boxed{\dot q=H_p.}
\]
This is one of Hamilton's equations, coming directly from the variation of momentum.

\subsection{Now vary position}
Let $q\mapsto q+\varepsilon\eta$, with $p$ fixed and $\eta(t_0)=\eta(t_1)=0$. Then
\[
 \delta_q S_H=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
 \left(p\dot\eta-H_q\eta\right)\dd t.
\]
Integrate the first term by parts:
\[
 \delta_q S_H=[p\eta]_{t_0}^{t_1}
 -\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\left(\dot p+H_q\right)\eta\dd t.
\]
The endpoint term vanishes, and hence
\[
 \boxed{\dot p=-H_q.}
\]

\begin{intuition}
This gives a second, especially beautiful answer to \emph{why those equations?} The phase-space action contains $p\dot q-H$. Vary $p$ and you get the velocity equation. Vary $q$ and, after integration by parts, you get the momentum equation and its minus sign. The whole pair still comes from stationary action.
\end{intuition}

\section{Is the Hamiltonian always the total energy?}
\subsection{Usually, in the examples you first meet}
For the common Lagrangian
\[
 L(q,\dot q)=\tfrac12m\dot q^2-V(q),
\]
we have shown $H=T+V$. For many particles with ordinary kinetic energy, the conclusion is similar. Energy conservation can then be understood through the Hamiltonian.

Differentiate $H(q(t),p(t),t)$ along a physical trajectory:
\begin{align*}
 \dt{H}
 &=H_q\dot q+H_p\dot p+H_t\\
 &=H_q H_p+H_p(-H_q)+H_t\\
 &=\boxed{H_t}.
\end{align*}
Thus, if $H$ has no explicit time dependence, it is conserved.

\subsection{Two distinctions that matter}
\textbf{First: conserved does not automatically mean ``mechanical energy''.} The Hamiltonian is defined by the Legendre transform, not by the phrase $T+V$. In standard time-independent natural coordinates the two often coincide, but in more general formulations the identification needs checking.

\textbf{Second: being an energy does not automatically mean being conserved.} Take
\[
 L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2-V(q,t),\quad
 H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q,t).
\]
This $H$ is still kinetic plus potential energy, yet it can change because $V$ changes explicitly with time:
\[
 \dt H=\pd{V}{t}.
\]
A time-dependent environment can supply or remove energy.

\begin{pausebox}
If $H(q,p)$ is constant along a trajectory, why does that not imply $q$ and $p$ are each constant? Because a point can move \emph{along a level curve} of a function while the value of that function stays unchanged. A swinging pendulum and a mass on a spring do exactly this.
\end{pausebox}

\section{Worked example I: the free particle}
This is the place to feel how little machinery is actually needed.

\eqstep{1. Write the Lagrangian.} For a particle on a frictionless line, choose the zero of potential energy so that
\[
 L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2.
\]
\eqstep{2. Identify conjugate momentum.}
\[
 p=\pd{L}{\dot q}=m\dot q,\qquad\dot q=\frac{p}{m}.
\]
\eqstep{3. Legendre transform.}
\[
 H=p\dot q-L=\frac{p^2}{2m}.
\]
\eqstep{4. Apply the two equations.}
\[
 \dot q=\pd{H}{p}=\frac{p}{m},\qquad
 \dot p=-\pd{H}{q}=0.
\]
\eqstep{5. Interpret.} $p$ stays fixed because $H$ has no $q$-dependence. With $p(t)=p_0$,
\[
 \boxed{q(t)=q_0+\frac{p_0}{m}\,t.}
\]
An unchanged momentum gives a constant velocity. This is Newton's first law in Hamiltonian language.

\begin{intuition}
Here a single glance at the shape of $H$ already reveals the motion. No $q$ in $H$ means no change in $p$. A quadratic dependence on $p$ means velocity is proportional to momentum.
\end{intuition}

\section{Worked example II: a mass on a spring}
A mass $m$ is attached to an ideal spring of stiffness $k$, with displacement $q$ measured from equilibrium.

\subsection{Construct the Hamiltonian}
The potential is $V(q)=\tfrac12kq^2$, so
\[
 L=\frac12m\dot q^2-\frac12kq^2,\qquad
 p=m\dot q.
\]
The Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:springH}
 \boxed{H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12kq^2.}
\end{equation}
Hamilton's equations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:springpair}
 \boxed{\dot q=\frac{p}{m},\qquad\dot p=-kq.}
\end{equation}

\subsection{See the motion without solving anything}
Suppose the mass is displaced to the right ($q>0$) and released ($p=0$). Then $\dot q=0$ at that instant, but $\dot p=-kq<0$: its momentum immediately begins becoming negative. It starts moving left.

At equilibrium ($q=0$), we have $\dot p=0$, yet momentum need not vanish. In fact, speed is greatest there. On the left ($q<0$), $\dot p>0$, and momentum eventually turns positive.

This describes oscillation in two lines. Position tells you how momentum is being changed by the spring; momentum tells you how position is changing.

\subsection{Connect to the familiar second-order equation}
Differentiate $\dot q=p/m$ with respect to time and insert $\dot p=-kq$:
\[
 \ddot q=\frac{\dot p}{m}=-\frac{k}{m}q,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad m\ddot q+kq=0.
\]
We have exactly the standard harmonic oscillator. Nothing was replaced physically: one second-order equation became two first-order equations.

With $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ and initial data $(q_0,p_0)$, its motion is
\begin{align*}
 q(t)&=q_0\cos(\omega t)+\frac{p_0}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t),\\
 p(t)&=p_0\cos(\omega t)-m\omega q_0\sin(\omega t).
\end{align*}
You do not need to memorise these expressions for the conceptual journey.

\subsection{Look at the motion in phase space}
Because $H$ is conserved, a physical state stays on
\[
 \frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac{kq^2}{2}=E.
\]
For fixed $E>0$, this is an \emph{ellipse} in the $(q,p)$ plane. The particle does not move along a line in this plane: as $q$ and $p$ change, the point runs around the ellipse.

\begin{intuition}
The energy equation gives the \emph{shape of the allowed path}. Hamilton's equations give its \emph{direction and speed of traversal}. The Hamiltonian is therefore more than a conserved number: through its derivatives it organises the entire motion.
\end{intuition}

\section{Worked example III: gravity along a vertical line}
Choose $q$ as height, positive upwards. In a uniform gravitational field,
\[
 V(q)=mgq,\qquad L=\tfrac12m\dot q^2-mgq.
\]
As usual, $p=m\dot q$, and
\[
 H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+mgq.
\]
The two equations are
\[
 \dot q=\frac{p}{m},\qquad\dot p=-mg.
\]
The second says the momentum decreases by $mg$ every second: gravity exerts a constant downward force. Therefore,
\[
 p(t)=p_0-mgt,
\qquad
 q(t)=q_0+\frac{p_0}{m}t-\frac12gt^2.
\]
This is precisely the familiar constant-acceleration motion.

There is an instructive contrast: for the free particle, $H$ had no $q$ term and momentum stayed fixed; adding the term $mgq$ creates a constant $q$-slope, so $p$ changes at a constant rate.

\section{Worked example IV: the pendulum and angular momentum}
A pendulum consists of a mass $m$ suspended from a light rod of length $\ell$. Let $\theta=0$ at the lowest point, and take the potential to vanish there.
\[
 T=\tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2,
\qquad V=mg\ell(1-\cos\theta).
\]
Define $I=m\ell^2$. The Lagrangian and conjugate momentum are
\[
 L=\tfrac12I\dot\theta^2-mg\ell(1-\cos\theta),
\qquad p_\theta=I\dot\theta.
\]
The Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pendH}
 \boxed{H(\theta,p_\theta)=\frac{p_\theta^2}{2I}+mg\ell(1-\cos\theta).}
\end{equation}
Its equations become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pendpair}
 \boxed{\dot\theta=\frac{p_\theta}{I},\qquad
 \dot p_\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta.}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Why this example matters}
The natural position variable is an \emph{angle}, and its natural momentum is \emph{angular momentum}. Nevertheless, Hamilton's equations have exactly the same form as for an ordinary particle on a line.

Use $p_\theta=I\dot\theta$ in $\dot p_\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta$ to recover
\[
 I\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta.
\]
For small angles, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and the pendulum behaves approximately like a harmonic oscillator. For larger angles the nonlinearity matters; Hamilton's equations themselves need no approximation.

\begin{pausebox}
At the turning point of a swinging pendulum, $\dot\theta=0$ so $p_\theta=0$. Does that mean the system is momentarily at equilibrium? No: unless $\sin\theta=0$, the angular momentum is \emph{changing} and the pendulum immediately reverses direction.
\end{pausebox}

\section{The deeper reason: generalised momentum is not always $m\dot q$}
\subsection{A coordinate-dependent kinetic coefficient}
Take a one-dimensional Lagrangian of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aql}
 L(q,\dot q)=\frac12a(q)\dot q^2-V(q),\qquad a(q)>0.
\end{equation}
You might encounter this because of a complicated coordinate system or a mechanical constraint. The key point is that the coefficient of $\dot q^2$ changes with position.

Directly from the definition,
\[
 p=\pd{L}{\dot q}=a(q)\dot q,
\qquad\dot q=\frac{p}{a(q)}.
\]
The Legendre transform is
\[
 \boxed{H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2a(q)}+V(q).}
\]
And Hamilton's equations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aqham}
 \boxed{\dot q=\frac{p}{a(q)},\qquad
 \dot p=\frac{a'(q)p^2}{2a(q)^2}-V'(q).}
\end{equation}
The momentum rate now has a term involving $a'(q)$. This is not an extra physical force magically introduced by the formalism. It accounts for the way the chosen coordinate measures motion differently at different positions.

\subsection{Check that it is exactly Euler--Lagrange}
Euler--Lagrange, applied directly to \eqref{eq:aql}, says
\[
 \dt{}\bigl(a(q)\dot q\bigr)=\tfrac12a'(q)\dot q^2-V'(q).
\]
Expand the derivative and rearrange:
\[
 a(q)\ddot q+\tfrac12a'(q)\dot q^2+V'(q)=0.
\]
Now take the Hamiltonian result, use $p=a(q)\dot q$, and compare:
\[
 \dot p=a(q)\ddot q+a'(q)\dot q^2
       =\tfrac12a'(q)\dot q^2-V'(q).
\]
They are identical.

\begin{intuition}
\textbf{This is the strongest reason not to define momentum by $m\dot q$ in advance.} In generalised coordinates the correct momentum is whatever the Lagrangian tells us via $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$. Hamiltonian mechanics works with that quantity directly, preserving the same simple pair of equations in very different settings.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{A useful extension: motion in polar coordinates}
For a mass $m$ moving in a plane under a central potential $V(r)$,
\[
 L=\tfrac12m\left(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2\right)-V(r).
\]
There are \emph{two} conjugate momenta:
\[
 p_r=m\dot r,\qquad p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta.
\]
The Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polarH}
 \boxed{H=\frac{p_r^2}{2m}+\frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}+V(r).}
\end{equation}
From its derivatives, all four equations follow:
\begin{align*}
 \dot r&=\frac{p_r}{m},
 &\dot\theta&=\frac{p_\theta}{mr^2},\\
 \dot p_r&=\frac{p_\theta^2}{mr^3}-V'(r),
 &\dot p_\theta&=0.
\end{align*}

The last equation is particularly satisfying: $H$ is independent of $\theta$, so $p_\theta$ is conserved. This is conservation of angular momentum emerging directly from the form of the Hamiltonian. The positive $p_\theta^2/(mr^3)$ term in the radial equation reflects radial motion in polar coordinates, not a newly added fundamental force.

\section{Phase space is more than a diagram}
\subsection{Energy contours and the direction of motion}
For a time-independent Hamiltonian $H(q,p)$, motion lies on a contour $H=E$. A tiny displacement $(\dd q,\dd p)$ tangent to such a contour obeys
\[
 H_q\dd q+H_p\dd p=0.
\]
Hamilton's equations prescribe
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}\dot q\\\dot p\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}H_q\\H_p\end{pmatrix}.
\]
In words, the velocity of the \emph{phase-space point} is obtained by turning the gradient of $H$ through a right angle, with a particular orientation. Thus the motion is automatically tangent to the level curves, rather than crossing them.

\subsection{Equilibrium is a stationary point of the Hamiltonian}
An equilibrium phase-space state satisfies $\dot q=\dot p=0$, so for a smooth one-coordinate Hamiltonian
\[
 H_p=0,\qquad H_q=0.
\]
For the spring $H=p^2/(2m)+kq^2/2$, the only equilibrium is $(q,p)=(0,0)$. The surrounding energy contours are closed, so small displacements cause oscillation rather than escape.

For the pendulum there are also upright equilibria at $\theta=\pi$ (modulo $2\pi$), which are unstable. The shape of the Hamiltonian, and not merely the individual differential equations, helps us understand these qualitative differences.

\section{A glimpse ahead: momentum can differ from mechanical momentum}
We have seen angular momentum and coordinate-dependent momentum. There is one further distinction worth knowing now, even if you postpone the algebra.

For a charged particle of charge $e$ in electromagnetic potentials $\boldsymbol A(q,t)$ and $\phi(q,t)$, a standard Lagrangian is
\[
 L=\frac12m\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol q}\rVert^2
 +e\boldsymbol A\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol q}-e\phi.
\]
Its canonical momentum is
\[
 \boxed{\boldsymbol p=m\dot{\boldsymbol q}+e\boldsymbol A.}
\]
The mechanical momentum is $m\dot{\boldsymbol q}$; the canonical momentum is $\boldsymbol p$. They need not be the same. The Hamiltonian is
\[
 H=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol p-e\boldsymbol A\rVert^2}{2m}+e\phi.
\]
And still $\dot{\boldsymbol q}=\partial H/\partial\boldsymbol p$.

This is precisely why the \emph{definition} $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$ is more general than the school-level formula $p=mv$. Hamiltonian mechanics is built around the momentum \emph{conjugate to the coordinate}, not a preconceived notion of linear momentum.

\section{A short preview: Poisson brackets}
You do not need this for the basics, but it reveals how tightly the structure fits together. For any smooth quantity $F(q,p,t)$, the chain rule and Hamilton's equations give
\begin{align*}
 \dt F
 &=F_q\dot q+F_p\dot p+F_t\\
 &=F_qH_p-F_pH_q+F_t.
\end{align*}
Define the \emph{Poisson bracket} of two functions by
\[
 \{F,G\}:=F_qG_p-F_pG_q.
\]
Then the entire time evolution of any observable can be written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PB}
 \boxed{\dt F=\{F,H\}+\pd{F}{t}.}
\end{equation}
For $F=q$ it gives $\dot q=H_p$; for $F=p$ it gives $\dot p=-H_q$. So the two Hamilton equations are special cases of a single rule describing how every physical quantity evolves.

\section{Guided practice: think before calculating}
Try each question first without manipulating equations. Then use the short solutions that follow.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Recognising what changes.} A Hamiltonian is $H(q,p)=p^2/(2m)+U_0$, where $U_0$ is constant. What happens to momentum? What does the particle do?
\item \textbf{Finding the force.} A particle has $H(q,p)=p^2/(2m)+\tfrac12kq^2+F_0q$. Write Hamilton's equations. What is the equilibrium position?
\item \textbf{Momentum is not always $m\dot q$.} A coordinate $x$ has $L=\tfrac12(1+x^2)\dot x^2-\tfrac12x^2$. Find $p$, eliminate $\dot x$, and construct $H(x,p)$.
\item \textbf{Conserved coordinate momentum.} If $H(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$ does not depend on $q_2$, which momentum is conserved? Which Hamilton equation tells you this?
\item \textbf{Energy versus conservation.} For $H(q,p,t)=p^2/(2m)+\tfrac12k(t)q^2$, is $H$ necessarily constant? What is $\dd H/\dd t$ along the motion?
\item \textbf{Qualitative pendulum.} At a pendulum's lowest point with $\theta=0$ and $p_\theta>0$, which way does its angle initially change? What is $\dot p_\theta$ at that precise instant?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Answers and the reason behind them}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Guided practice answers}
\textbf{1.} $\dot p=-H_q=0$, and $\dot q=p/m$ is constant. The additive constant $U_0$ does not affect the motion.

\textbf{2.} $\dot q=p/m$ and $\dot p=-kq-F_0$. Equilibrium requires $p=0$ and $-kq-F_0=0$, hence $q_{\rm eq}=-F_0/k$. Adding a linear potential term changes the force by a constant.

\textbf{3.} $p=(1+x^2)\dot x$, so $\dot x=p/(1+x^2)$ and
\[
 H(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{2(1+x^2)}+\frac12x^2.
\]
The coordinate-dependent kinetic coefficient has been transferred to the denominator of the momentum term.

\textbf{4.} $\dot p_2=-\partial H/\partial q_2=0$, so $p_2$ is conserved. This is the simplest form of the connection between an absent coordinate (a \emph{cyclic coordinate}) and a conserved conjugate momentum.

\textbf{5.} Not necessarily: $\dd H/\dd t=\partial H/\partial t=\tfrac12k'(t)q^2$. Changing the spring stiffness externally can do work on the system.

\textbf{6.} $\dot\theta=p_\theta/I>0$, so the angle is increasing. At $\theta=0$, $\dot p_\theta=-mg\ell\sin0=0$. A zero \emph{instantaneous rate of change of momentum} does not imply zero momentum.

\section{One-page mental map}
\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The input from Lagrangian mechanics}
\[
 S[q]=\int L(q,\dot q,t)\dd t,
 \qquad \dt{}\left(\pd{L}{\dot q}\right)=\pd{L}{q}.
\]
\textbf{What the action tells us to call momentum}
\[
 \boxed{p=\pd{L}{\dot q}.}
\]
\textbf{What we want to achieve}

Use $(q,p)$ to describe the current state and obtain its two rates of change directly.

\textbf{How we change variables}
\[
 \boxed{H(q,p,t)=p\dot q-L(q,\dot q,t),\quad
        \dot q=\dot q(q,p,t).}
\]
\textbf{What follows (not what we must guess)}
\[
 \boxed{\dot q=\pd{H}{p},\qquad\dot p=-\pd{H}{q}.}
\]
\textbf{Why it works}

The unwanted $\dd\dot q$ terms cancel in $\dd H$ because $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$; the remaining $q$ derivative agrees with Euler--Lagrange. Alternatively, both equations come from stationarity of $\int(p\dot q-H)\dd t$.

\textbf{How to read it}

Momentum determines how position changes. Position determines how momentum changes. Taken together, these rates move the state through phase space.
\end{keybox}

\subsection*{Five ideas to retain}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=22pt]
 \item Hamiltonian mechanics describes the \emph{same physical motion} as Newtonian and Lagrangian mechanics.
 \item The key variable is \emph{conjugate} momentum, $p=L_{\dot q}$, not always $m\dot q$.
 \item The Legendre transform exchanges velocity for momentum by using the slope of $L$ as the new variable.
 \item Hamilton's crossed, opposite-sign equations are forced by that transform and the variational principle.
 \item The Hamiltonian often equals total energy, but its \emph{definition} is $\sum p_i\dot q_i-L$, and its chief job is to generate motion.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Final perspective}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Final perspective}
Lagrangian mechanics asks which entire path makes the action stationary. Hamiltonian mechanics asks how the present state $(q,p)$ flows into the next state. It has not abandoned the action principle: it has reorganised it, exposing a paired structure that was present all along.

The most important step is the least arbitrary one. Because the action already gives $\partial L/\partial\dot q$ a special role, we call it momentum. Because we want a function of this momentum, we perform a Legendre transform. And because of how that transform works, Hamilton's equations follow.

\begin{intuition}[title={\strut The thought worth taking away}]
\emph{Velocity tells us how position is changing. Conjugate momentum tells us which velocity-related quantity the action singles out. Hamiltonian mechanics uses that natural momentum so that one function can describe both the evolution of position and the evolution of momentum.}
\end{intuition}
